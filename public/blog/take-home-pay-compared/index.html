Take-Home Pay Compared: UK vs USA vs Australia vs Canada on the Same Salary

Published 18 August 2026 · 9 min read · By Tabutility

Same job title. Same experience level. Radically different paychecks. If you've ever wondered what $75,000 — or its rough equivalent — actually means in your bank account depending on where you live, you've landed in the right place.

Tax systems vary wildly across countries. Income tax rates differ. Social contributions go by different names and different rates. Healthcare might be funded through your paycheck or billed separately. Retirement savings might be compulsory. The result is that two people earning the "same" salary can take home amounts that differ by tens of thousands a year — and that gap has enormous implications for career decisions, international moves, and financial planning.

We ran benchmark salaries through four major English-speaking tax systems — the UK, USA, Australia, and Canada — to give you a clear, side-by-side picture.

The Benchmark Salaries

Because exchange rates fluctuate and purchasing power differs, we chose salaries that represent a broadly equivalent mid-career, professional-level income in each country:

  • UK: £50,000 (2024/25 tax year)
  • USA: $75,000 (2024 federal + typical state)
  • Australia: A$80,000 (2024/25 tax year)
  • Canada: C$80,000 (2024 federal + provincial)

These aren't perfect dollar-for-dollar equivalents — but they are realistic mid-career salaries in each country's professional job market.

🇬🇧 United Kingdom: £50,000

The UK tax system combines income tax with National Insurance (NI) — a payroll contribution that funds the NHS and state pension. For 2024/25:

  • Income tax: 0% on the first £12,570 (personal allowance), 20% on £12,571–£50,270, 40% on anything above — at £50,000 you stay just below the 40% threshold on most income
  • National Insurance (Class 1): 8% on earnings between £12,570–£50,268, then 2% above

On £50,000, you pay roughly £7,486 in income tax and approximately £4,800 in NI contributions, leaving you with around £37,500–£37,700 net per year (about 75% of gross). Use our UK Salary Calculator to model your exact take-home with pension contributions and other factors.

The key thing to understand about NI: it's a payroll deduction that directly funds public healthcare. UK workers pay less out-of-pocket for healthcare than their US counterparts — that NI money is doing real work.

🇺🇸 United States: $75,000

The US is unique among these four countries in the degree to which your state of residence affects your final paycheck. There's federal income tax, FICA (Social Security + Medicare), and then state income tax — which ranges from 0% to over 13%.

  • Federal income tax: Progressive rates — 10%, 12%, 22%, 24% brackets. On $75,000 (single filer, standard deduction), effective federal rate is roughly 13–14%
  • FICA: 6.2% Social Security (up to the wage cap) + 1.45% Medicare = 7.65% total
  • State income tax: $0 in Texas, Florida, Nevada, Wyoming, South Dakota, Alaska, Washington; up to ~6–9% in California, New York, New Jersey

Result: take-home on $75,000 ranges from roughly $61,000 in a no-tax state to around $55,000 in a high-tax state — a $6,000+ swing purely based on ZIP code. Explore the full state-by-state picture in our US state-by-state data story, or calculate your own scenario at the US Salary Hub.

⚠️ The US figure doesn't include health insurance premiums. If your employer doesn't fully cover health insurance, you may pay $3,000–$8,000/year in premiums — which can eliminate the apparent tax advantage over the UK.

🇦🇺 Australia: A$80,000

Australia's income tax system is administered by the ATO and is relatively straightforward — a single national tax rate (no state income tax), plus a 2% Medicare levy that funds public healthcare (Medicare).

  • Income tax: 0% up to A$18,200, then tiered up to 19% → 32.5% → 37% → 45%; at A$80,000 the marginal rate is 32.5%
  • Medicare levy: 2% flat on taxable income
  • Low Income Tax Offset (LITO): phases out above A$37,500, but still partially applies at A$80,000

On A$80,000, you pay roughly A$16,467 in income tax and A$1,600 in Medicare levy, giving a net of approximately A$61,900 — about 77.4% of gross. See a full state-by-state comparison in our Australian states data story, or run your own numbers at the AU Salary Hub.

There is one critical addition: Superannuation. Your employer must contribute 11.5% of your gross salary (2024/25 rate) into a retirement fund on top of your salary — that's A$9,200/year in super you're building alongside your cash pay. This is not deducted from your paycheck, but it is very real compensation that the headline net figure doesn't capture.

🇨🇦 Canada: C$80,000

Canada has the most complex system of these four, because each province sets its own income tax rates in addition to federal tax. Federal deductions include CPP (Canada Pension Plan) and EI (Employment Insurance).

  • Federal income tax: 15% on the first C$55,867, 20.5% on C$55,867–C$111,733 (at C$80,000, your marginal federal rate is 20.5%)
  • CPP contributions: approximately C$3,867 (2024)
  • EI premiums: approximately C$1,049 (2024)
  • Provincial income tax: ranges from ~6% (Alberta — no provincial sales tax) to ~18%+ (Quebec)

Take-home on C$80,000 ranges from roughly C$59,000–C$67,000 in lower-tax provinces like Alberta and Ontario, to around C$56,000 in Quebec which has the highest combined provincial burden. What the Numbers Actually Mean

The raw percentages tell only part of the story. Here's what each country's deductions are actually buying you:

  • UK (NI): National Insurance funds the NHS — meaning GP visits, hospital stays, and most specialist care are free at the point of use. The ~8% NI bite is, in many ways, a healthcare premium with very broad coverage.
  • USA (FICA): Social Security contributions fund your eventual retirement benefit and survivors/disability insurance. Medicare (1.45%) funds public healthcare for over-65s only — working-age Americans typically need private health insurance, which can cost $300–$700+/month for a single person.
  • Australia (Super): The compulsory 11.5% superannuation contribution sits outside your salary entirely — you don't feel it as a deduction, but it is building a retirement pot in your name. Australia's Medicare levy (2%) funds a universal public healthcare system similar in principle to the NHS.
  • Canada (CPP/EI): CPP builds your state pension; EI funds unemployment insurance. Provincial variation in income tax reflects that provinces fund their own education and healthcare — Quebec's higher taxes buy an extensive public services network including subsidised childcare.

What This Means for Your Career

If you're considering an international move or a remote role that lets you choose your tax base, the differences above are material — potentially $5,000–$10,000+ per year. A few practical takeaways:

  1. Don't ignore healthcare costs when comparing US offers to other countries. A $75,000 US offer with $6,000/year in health premiums may net less than a £50,000 UK salary with NHS coverage.
  2. Australian super is hidden compensation. If you're comparing an AU offer to a US offer, add 11.5% to the Australian gross to get total-comp parity.
  3. Canadian province matters enormously. A job offer in Alberta and the same offer in Quebec can differ by C$6,000–C$8,000/year in take-home on the same gross.
  4. US state selection is the biggest lever. Choosing Texas over California on a $75,000 salary saves roughly $3,000–$5,000/year in state income tax alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which country has the highest take-home pay?

On broadly equivalent mid-career salaries, the USA typically returns the highest percentage of gross pay — around 73–81% depending on the state — because federal income tax rates start low and there is no mandatory national healthcare levy. States with no income tax (Texas, Florida, Nevada, etc.) push take-home even higher. Australia comes second at roughly 77% on cash take-home before factoring in super, followed by Canada (70–84% depending on province) and the UK (73–75%).

Does the USA really have lower taxes than the UK?

At a mid-career salary level, the effective combined rate of US federal income tax plus FICA is typically lower than the UK's combined income tax plus National Insurance. However, US workers often pay significant amounts for private health insurance that the UK's NHS covers through NI, so the real disposable income gap is narrower than the headline tax rates suggest. Once you account for healthcare costs, the two countries are often comparable in true disposable income.

How does Australian superannuation affect take-home pay?

Superannuation (super) is a compulsory employer contribution of 11.5% of your gross salary in 2024/25 — it is paid on top of your salary, not deducted from it. This means your cash take-home pay is calculated on the headline salary alone, but you are also accumulating retirement savings simultaneously. When super is factored in as total compensation, Australia's effective return is significantly higher than the cash take-home figure alone suggests.
