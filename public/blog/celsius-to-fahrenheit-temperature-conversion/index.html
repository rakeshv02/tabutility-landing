Celsius to Fahrenheit: How Temperature Conversion Works

Published 28 July 2026
Home › Blog › Celsius to Fahrenheit Temperature Conversion

The Three Temperature Scales

Three temperature scales are in common use today. Celsius (°C) is the standard in science and most of the world. Fahrenheit (°F) is used primarily in the United States and a handful of other countries for everyday weather and cooking. Kelvin (K) is the SI base unit used in physics and chemistry — it starts at absolute zero, the coldest possible temperature.

The Conversion Formulas

Celsius to Fahrenheit

°F = (°C × 9/5) + 32

Example: 20°C → (20 × 9/5) + 32 = 36 + 32 = 68°F

Fahrenheit to Celsius

°C = (°F − 32) × 5/9

Example: 98.6°F → (98.6 − 32) × 5/9 = 66.6 × 0.5556 = 37°C (normal body temperature)

Celsius to Kelvin

K = °C + 273.15

Example: 0°C (freezing point of water) = 273.15 K

Fahrenheit to Kelvin

K = (°F + 459.67) × 5/9

The Mental Maths Shortcut

For quick estimates when you don't have a calculator:

  • Celsius → Fahrenheit: Double the Celsius value, then add 30.
    Example: 25°C → (25×2) + 30 = 80°F (exact: 77°F). Good enough for everyday use.
  • Fahrenheit → Celsius: Subtract 30, then halve it.
    Example: 80°F → (80−30) ÷ 2 = 25°C (exact: 26.7°C).

These shortcuts are within 2–4°F accuracy for typical weather and body temperatures — not precise enough for cooking or science, but fine for "is it warm enough for a T-shirt?"

Key Anchor Points to Memorise

  • 0°C = 32°F — water freezes
  • 20°C ≈ 68°F — pleasantly warm room / spring day
  • 37°C = 98.6°F — healthy human body temperature
  • 100°C = 212°F — water boils
  • −40°C = −40°F — where the scales meet

Why Two Scales? A Brief History

Gabriel Fahrenheit (1686–1736) was a German physicist who developed his scale in 1724. He set 0°F at the temperature of an ice/salt/water mixture (the coldest he could reliably create in a lab) and calibrated 96°F to body temperature. Later adjustments moved body temperature to 98.6°F.

Anders Celsius (1701–1744) proposed the centigrade scale in 1742 — 0° for the boiling point of water and 100° for freezing. Confusingly, this was inverted from today's convention; it was reversed after his death to the form we use today.

Lord Kelvin (William Thomson, 1824–1907) established the absolute scale based on thermodynamic principles — 0 K is absolute zero, the theoretical minimum temperature at which all molecular motion stops.

FAQ

What is 100°C in Fahrenheit?

100°C is exactly 212°F — the boiling point of water at sea level (1 atm pressure). At altitude, where air pressure is lower, water boils at a lower temperature — at the summit of Everest (~8,849m), water boils at approximately 70°C (158°F), which is why cooking times must be extended significantly at high altitude.

What is the quick mental shortcut for Celsius to Fahrenheit?

Double the Celsius value and add 30. For 20°C: (20×2)+30 = 70°F (exact answer is 68°F). This estimate is accurate to within 3–4°F for temperatures between -10°C and 40°C — the range you encounter in everyday life. Not precise enough for medical or cooking use, but excellent for getting a feel for weather forecasts.

Why does the US still use Fahrenheit?

The US adopted Fahrenheit as its standard before the Metric Conversion Act of 1975 established a national policy of voluntary metrication. Unlike most countries, the US never mandated metric adoption. The Fahrenheit scale arguably has some practical appeal for human-scale weather — 0°F is bitterly cold and 100°F is dangerously hot, giving intuitive meaning to the range — but scientifically and globally, Celsius is far more practical.

What temperature is the same in Celsius and Fahrenheit?

−40°. At exactly −40°C = −40°F the two scales intersect. You can verify with the formula: (−40 × 9/5) + 32 = −72 + 32 = −40. This is also approximately the temperature of extremely cold winter days in interior Alaska, northern Canada, and Siberia.
