How Much Water Should You Drink Per Day? The Real Answer

Published 28 July 2026
Home › Blog › How Much Water Should You Drink Per Day?

Where Does the "8 Glasses a Day" Rule Come From?

The "8×8" recommendation — eight 8-ounce glasses per day, totalling about 1.9 litres — has been repeated so often it feels scientific. In reality, it has almost no clinical basis. It appears to have originated from a 1945 US dietary recommendation that was widely misquoted and has persisted through decades of health messaging.

The actual science is more nuanced: your hydration needs vary significantly based on your body weight, activity level, diet, climate, and health status.

How to Calculate Your Personal Water Intake

The most widely cited evidence-based formula used by dietitians is:

Daily water (ml) = body weight (kg) × 35

So a 70kg adult needs approximately 70 × 35 = 2,450 ml (2.45 litres) as a baseline.

Adjustments to make:

  • Exercise — add 500–750ml per hour of moderate exercise; more for intense or prolonged activity in heat.
  • Hot climate — add 500–1,000ml on hot days or if you work in a hot environment.
  • Pregnancy — NHS recommends an additional 300ml per day; breastfeeding requires roughly an additional 500–700ml.
  • High-fibre diet — fibre absorbs water; increase intake slightly if you eat a high-fibre diet.
  • Illness with fever, vomiting, or diarrhoea — significant extra hydration needed; oral rehydration salts may be appropriate.

Does Coffee and Tea Count?

Yes. Despite the common myth, caffeinated drinks do count towards daily fluid intake. While caffeine has a mild diuretic effect, research shows that regular tea and coffee drinkers are fully adapted to this effect, and the net fluid contribution is positive.

A 250ml cup of coffee contributes roughly 200–220ml of effective hydration. Alcohol is a different matter — it actively increases water loss and should not be counted.

Signs You're Drinking Too Little

  • Dark yellow urine — aim for pale straw yellow. Dark yellow or amber means you need more water.
  • Thirst — by the time you feel thirsty, you're already mildly dehydrated (1–2% body weight deficit).
  • Headaches and fatigue — often the first cognitive effects of mild dehydration.
  • Dry lips and mouth — basic indicator of inadequate fluid intake.
  • Reduced urination — fewer than 4 bathroom visits per day is a sign of underhydration.

Can You Drink Too Much Water?

Hyponatremia — low blood sodium caused by excessive water intake — is rare but real. It occurs when you dilute sodium levels by drinking large volumes without electrolytes. Symptoms include nausea, headache, confusion, and in severe cases, seizures.

For healthy adults in normal daily life, the kidneys can process up to about 1 litre per hour. The risk is primarily relevant during endurance events (marathons, ultramarathons, triathlons) where athletes over-drink plain water for hours. Electrolyte drinks are recommended for events lasting over 90 minutes.

Practical Tips for Staying Hydrated

  • Start each morning with a large glass of water before coffee — your body is mildly dehydrated after 7–8 hours of sleep.
  • Keep a 500ml or 1-litre bottle on your desk as a visual cue.
  • Eat water-rich foods: cucumber (96% water), celery (95%), tomatoes (94%), watermelon (92%), strawberries (91%).
  • Check your urine colour mid-afternoon as a daily audit.
  • Set reminder alarms if you frequently forget to drink during focused work.

FAQ

Is 2 litres of water a day enough?

For a sedentary adult in a temperate climate, 2 litres of total fluid intake is roughly adequate. Active people, those in hot climates, pregnant or breastfeeding women, and larger individuals need considerably more — often 3–4 litres or higher when exercise and environment are factored in.

Does coffee count towards daily water intake?

Yes. Despite caffeine's mild diuretic effect, tea and coffee still contribute positively to net fluid intake. The water in the drink outweighs the diuretic effect. However, very high caffeine consumption (more than 6 cups of strong coffee per day) may begin to have a net negative effect on hydration.

What are the signs of dehydration?

Early signs include dark yellow urine, persistent thirst, dry mouth, and fatigue. Moderate dehydration causes headaches, difficulty concentrating, dizziness, and constipation. Severe dehydration (5%+ body weight deficit) is a medical emergency requiring prompt fluid replacement.

Can you drink too much water?

Yes — hyponatremia (low blood sodium from dilution) can occur when very large volumes of plain water are consumed rapidly. In everyday settings this is rare in healthy adults. The main risk is during prolonged endurance events. Use electrolyte drinks for exercise lasting over 90 minutes in warm conditions.
