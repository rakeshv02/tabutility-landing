How to Calculate Compound Interest:
The Eighth Wonder of the World

8 min read · Calculators · Updated July 2026

Albert Einstein is often credited with calling compound interest "the eighth wonder of the world — he who understands it, earns it; he who doesn't, pays it." Whether he actually said it is debated, but the sentiment is spot-on. Compound interest is one of the most powerful forces in personal finance, and understanding it can be the difference between building real wealth and paying off debt forever.

This guide explains exactly how compound interest works, shows you the formula step-by-step, and walks through real examples so you can see the numbers yourself.

What Is Compound Interest?

Simple interest earns a return only on your original principal. Compound interest earns a return on your principal plus all previously earned interest. That distinction seems small at first, but over time it creates an enormous gap.

Think of it like a snowball rolling downhill. The larger the snowball gets, the more snow it picks up per revolution. Your investment works the same way — the more you accumulate, the faster it grows.

The Compound Interest Formula

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)
Where:
A = Final amount · P = Principal · r = Annual interest rate (decimal)
n = Compounding frequency per year · t = Time in years

Breaking down each variable:

  • P (Principal) — The starting amount you deposit or invest.
  • r (Rate) — The annual interest rate expressed as a decimal (e.g. 5% = 0.05).
  • n (Frequency) — How many times interest compounds per year. Monthly = 12, quarterly = 4, annually = 1.
  • t (Time) — The number of years your money is invested.
  • A (Amount) — The total value at the end, including all compounded interest.

Step-by-Step Example

Scenario: You invest £5,000 at an annual interest rate of 7%, compounded monthly, for 20 years.

Step 1: Convert rate to decimal: 7% ÷ 100 = 0.07
Step 2: Identify n = 12 (monthly), t = 20
Step 3: A = 5,000 × (1 + 0.07/12)^(12×20)
Step 4: A = 5,000 × (1.005833)^240
Step 5: A = 5,000 × 4.0387 = £20,193

Your £5,000 grows to over £20,000 — you earned £15,193 in interest alone, without touching it.

How Compounding Frequency Changes Everything

The same £5,000 at 7% over 20 years, but with different compounding frequencies:

Daily compounding beats annual compounding by nearly £900 on just £5,000 — and that gap widens dramatically at larger amounts or longer time periods.

The Rule of 72

Want a quick mental estimate of how long it takes to double your money? Divide 72 by your annual interest rate.

  • At 6% — your money doubles in 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years
  • At 9% — your money doubles in 72 ÷ 9 = 8 years
  • At 12% — your money doubles in 72 ÷ 12 = 6 years

The Rule of 72 works in both directions. It also tells you how quickly debt grows if you don't pay it down — a credit card charging 24% interest doubles what you owe in just 3 years.

Why Time Is Your Biggest Asset

"The best time to plant a tree was 20 years ago. The second best time is now."

Consider two investors, both earning 8% per year compounded annually:

  • Alex invests £3,000/year from age 25–35 (10 years), then stops. Total invested: £30,000.
  • Sam invests £3,000/year from age 35–65 (30 years). Total invested: £90,000.

At age 65, Alex ends up with approximately £472,000 — Sam ends up with approximately £367,000. Alex invested a third of the money but started 10 years earlier. Time in the market beats timing the market.

Where Compound Interest Works Against You

The same mathematics that grows your savings also grows your debt. Credit cards, payday loans, and buy-now-pay-later schemes all use compound interest — sometimes daily. A £2,000 credit card balance at 22% APR compounded monthly grows to over £5,300 in 5 years if you make no payments.

This is why high-interest debt should always be the first financial priority — the compound effect is working in reverse on every outstanding balance.

Practical Tips for Maximising Compound Growth

  1. Start immediately — even a small amount benefits enormously from extra time.
  2. Reinvest all returns — dividends, interest, and gains should compound, not be withdrawn.
  3. Look for higher compounding frequency — monthly beats quarterly beats annually.
  4. Minimise fees — a 1% annual management fee can reduce your final pot by 20–25% over 30 years.
  5. Use tax-advantaged accounts — ISAs and SIPPs in the UK let compound interest work without tax drag.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good compound interest rate?

A "good" rate depends on the context. For a savings account in 2026, 4–5% is strong. For long-term stock market investments, historical averages suggest 7–10% annually (before inflation). For debt, any rate is bad — prioritise eliminating it.

Does compound interest apply to stocks?

Not exactly in the same formula, but the principle applies. When dividends are reinvested and share prices appreciate, the overall return compounds. Index funds that reinvest dividends are one of the most common ways to benefit from compounding.

How do I calculate compound interest in a spreadsheet?

In Excel or Google Sheets, use: =P*(1+r/n)^(n*t) where you replace each variable with its cell reference. Or use the FV function: =FV(r/n, n*t, 0, -P).

Is compound interest the same as APY?

APY (Annual Percentage Yield) accounts for compounding and gives you the true annual return. APR (Annual Percentage Rate) does not account for compounding within the year. Always compare savings accounts using APY, not APR.
