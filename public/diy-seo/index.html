The complete checklist

9 steps — in the order that matters

Work through these once and you've done what most agencies do in the first month of a retainer.

1

Set up Google Search Console Free from Google

What agencies call it: Monthly reporting dashboard

This is the single most important tool in SEO — and it's free from Google itself. It shows you which searches bring people to your site, which pages rank, how many people click, and any indexing problems Google has found. The monthly PDF agencies send you is mostly a screenshot of this dashboard.

Go to search.google.com/search-console, add your domain, and verify ownership (usually one DNS TXT record — your registrar's help pages explain it). Check it once a week. The queries where you rank position 5–20 are your biggest quick wins: those pages are close to page one and improving them moves the needle fastest.

2

See exactly how you appear in Google — then fix it

What agencies call it: On-page optimisation · $75–200/page

Your title tag and meta description are your listing in search results — they're your ad. If the title is cut off or the description is auto-generated filler, fewer people click even when you rank. Agencies bill per page for fixing this, because it genuinely matters: click-through rate directly affects how Google judges your relevance.

The snippet preview tool shows your title and description at Google's exact pixel limits in real time. Check every important page: does the title lead with the keyword? Does the description give someone a real reason to click over the results above and below you?

SERP Snippet Preview
3

Get your meta tags right on every page

What agencies call it: Technical audit finding · billed as part of retainer

Beyond your title and description, every page needs a canonical URL (tells Google which version of a page is the "real" one, preventing duplicate content problems) and Open Graph tags (controls how links look when shared on social media, Slack, WhatsApp). Missing or duplicated canonical tags are one of the most common paid-audit findings.

Fill in the generator, copy the HTML block it produces, and paste it into the <head> of each page. It takes two minutes per page and eliminates a whole category of technical issues.

Meta Tags Generator
4

Add structured data so Google understands your pages

What agencies call it: Schema markup implementation · $75–150/page

Schema markup is machine-readable code you add to a page that tells Google exactly what it is: a recipe, a product, a local business, a review, an FAQ. It doesn't directly boost rankings, but it helps Google understand your content better — and depending on the type and your site, it can make your listing eligible for rich results (review stars, breadcrumbs, product details) that take up more space in search results.

Pick your page type, fill in the fields, paste the generated JSON-LD into the page, and validate it with Google's free Rich Results Test.

Schema Markup Generator
5

Check your content before publishing

What agencies call it: Content optimisation · $100–500/article

Google rewards pages that cover a topic properly and read naturally. The two most common content problems: repeating a keyword so often it reads like spam (keyword stuffing), and writing in long, dense paragraphs that readers abandon. Content optimisation is a core retainer line item because it directly affects rankings and the time people spend on your pages.

Paste your draft in — if a single keyword dominates unnaturally, it will read like spam to people and to Google alike. There is no magic density target: cover the topic properly, write naturally, and keep the readability score at a level your actual audience can follow.

6

Fix your hreflang if you serve multiple countries Skip if single-country

What agencies call it: International SEO · premium audit line item

If you have pages for different countries or languages — a UK version and a US version, or an English and a Spanish site — hreflang annotations tell Google which page to show to which audience. They are notoriously easy to get wrong. Missing return links and invalid language codes are consistently among the most common errors in professional technical audits, and the result is Google showing the wrong version to the wrong country.

Generate the correct tag set for your language/country pairs, add them to your pages, then run the validator on your live URLs to confirm nothing is missing or broken.

7

Submit a sitemap and keep it current Free

What agencies call it: Indexation health · monthly monitoring

A sitemap is a file listing every URL you want Google to know about. Most website platforms (WordPress, Shopify, Wix, Squarespace) generate one automatically at /sitemap.xml. If yours doesn't, free sitemap generators can create one.

In Search Console, go to Sitemaps and submit your URL once — Google re-fetches it automatically. Every time you publish something new: make sure it appears in your sitemap, and link to it from at least one existing page. Internal links are how Google (and visitors) discover new content. A page with no inbound links is nearly invisible.

8

Earn links — the honest way

What agencies call it: Link building · $100–500+ per link

Links from other websites are Google's biggest trust signal — a vote that says your content is worth referencing. Link building is the one part of SEO where agencies genuinely do hard work: outreach, relationship-building, digital PR. It is also where the most abuse happens, which is why Google penalizes bought links and link schemes.

The honest version: publish something genuinely useful — original data, a free tool, a definitive guide — then tell the right people about it. Share in relevant communities, email journalists covering your niche, pitch guest posts. One good piece of content that earns ten real links beats a hundred bought links that can tank your rankings.

9

Review monthly — feed the winners, not everything equally

What agencies call it: Monthly reporting & strategy

This is what the monthly PDF is actually paying for. Once a month, open Search Console and ask two questions: which pages are gaining impressions, and which queries rank position 5–15 (just off the first page or first place)? Those are your best opportunities — improve the content, sharpen the title, add schema, get a link or two.

Spreading effort evenly across all pages is a waste. SEO compounds when you double down on what's already working. An hour a month of focused attention on your top 5 pages beats a week of general effort spread thin.

Common questions

Can I really do SEO myself without an agency?

Yes — for most small websites, the bulk of what an agency delivers is technical hygiene (titles, descriptions, structured data, sitemaps), content improvement, and monitoring. All of it can be done yourself with free tools and a few hours a month. Agencies add the most value for large sites, competitive industries, and systematic link-building campaigns.

How much do SEO agencies charge?

Typical retainers run $1,000–5,000/month for small-to-medium businesses, with one-off audits at $500–2,000 and content at $100–500 per article. Freelancers charge less; big-brand agencies charge far more. Much of the underlying work uses the same checks available in free tools.

How long does DIY SEO take to show results?

Technical fixes (titles, snippets, structured data) can be re-crawled within days to weeks. Ranking improvements from new content typically take 2–6 months, because Google needs time to discover, index, and build trust in pages. SEO compounds — the work you do now keeps paying off long after you do it.

What free tools do I need for DIY SEO?

Google Search Console (from Google, free) for monitoring, plus on-page utilities: a SERP snippet preview, meta tags generator, schema markup generator, keyword density and readability checker, and hreflang tools if you serve multiple countries. Tabutility provides all of these free with no sign-up required.

What parts of SEO are hard to do yourself?

Link building is the most labour-intensive part, and where agencies spend real effort. Competitive keyword research at scale and systematic digital PR are also harder solo. But most sites lose rankings to missing basics — get steps 1–7 right first; most sites never need much beyond that.
