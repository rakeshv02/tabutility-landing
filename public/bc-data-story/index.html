🍁 BC vs Every Province: Take-Home Pay on the Same Salary

The same gross salary, computed through every province's 2025 tax system. See how much British Columbia residents keep compared to Alberta, Ontario, Quebec and every other province — at four key salary levels.

The headline surprises many people: BC offers the highest take-home pay in Canada for salaries between C$80,000 and C$150,000. That's not because BC has low tax rates — it doesn't. It's because BC eliminated the Medical Services Plan (MSP) premium in 2020, has no provincial surtax, and its lowest bracket rate (5.06%) is competitive. Alberta edges ahead at lower incomes thanks to its large basic personal amount (C$21,872), but BC catches up quickly as income rises.

Every figure on this page is computed from 2025 federal and provincial tax rules. We use a single employed person with no deductions beyond the basic personal amount — a clean, apples-to-apples comparison across all 10 provinces.

Key findings

    Why BC ranks so highly

    Three structural reasons explain BC's strong after-tax performance at middle and higher incomes:

    1. No MSP premium. BC eliminated its Medical Services Plan premium in January 2020. Ontario charges up to C$900/year in health premiums; Alberta never had one. BC residents simply don't pay it.
    2. No provincial surtax. Ontario applies a surtax of 20% on provincial income tax above C$5,315 and an additional 36% above C$6,802. PEI adds a 10% surtax above C$12,000 of provincial tax. BC has neither. At higher incomes, these surtaxes compound significantly.
    3. Low entry-rate brackets. BC's bottom bracket rate (5.06%) is among the lowest in Canada. For the first C$45,654 of taxable income, BC charges less than almost every other province except Alberta. This benefits all earners, not just high ones.

    The catch: BC's top marginal rate of 20.5% (above C$240,716) is the second-highest in Canada, behind only Nova Scotia's 21%. Very high earners (C$300k+) do pay a premium for living in BC versus Alberta. But for the C$60k–C$150k range where most Canadians sit, BC is consistently the most tax-efficient province.

    Methodology

    All figures are computed using 2025 tax parameters published by the CRA and each provincial revenue authority. The calculations include:

    • Federal income tax — 2025 brackets: 15% / 20.5% / 26% / 29% / 33%. Basic personal amount C$16,129 (15% credit).
    • CPP — 5.95% on earnings between C$3,500 and C$68,500 pensionable earnings (all provinces except Quebec).
    • QPP (Quebec) — 6.4% on the same band; replaces CPP for Quebec residents.
    • EI — 1.66% on insurable earnings up to C$63,200 (all provinces except Quebec, which pays 1.31% plus QPIP at 0.494% up to C$88,000).
    • Provincial income tax — each province's 2025 brackets, basic personal amount, applicable surtaxes (Ontario, PEI), health premiums (Ontario), and the Quebec federal abatement (16.5% reduction in basic federal tax).

    We do not model RRSP contributions, pension deductions, spousal credits, or employer benefits. For a personalised figure, use the Canada Income Tax Calculator.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does BC have higher take-home pay than Alberta?

    At salaries of C$80,000 and above, yes — BC comes out ahead. At C$60,000 or below, Alberta edges BC by a small margin (about C$260/year at C$60k) because Alberta's very large basic personal amount (C$21,872) creates a bigger non-taxable band. Above C$80,000, BC's lower bracket rates and no MSP premium/surtax make it the top province.

    Which Canadian province has the highest take-home pay?

    British Columbia for salaries between C$80,000 and approximately C$150,000. Alberta takes the top spot below C$70,000 and again at very high incomes (C$300k+) where its 10%–15% flat rate beats BC's 20.5% top rate. Nova Scotia and PEI consistently rank last.

    How much less do you keep in Quebec vs BC?

    On a C$100,000 salary, Quebec residents keep approximately C$4,364/year less than BC residents. At C$120,000, the gap widens to about C$5,422. Quebec's rates go up to 25.75% and while the 16.5% federal abatement partially offsets this, it does not eliminate the gap.

    Why does Nova Scotia have the lowest take-home pay?

    Nova Scotia has a low basic personal amount (C$8,481), combined with tax rates up to 21% that kick in relatively early. The result is the highest effective combined tax burden in Canada at almost every income level. At C$100,000, Nova Scotia residents keep about C$6,700 less than BC residents annually.

    Does this include CPP and EI?

    Yes. CPP (5.95%, max C$68,500 pensionable) and EI (1.66%, max C$63,200 insurable) are included for all provinces. Quebec residents pay QPP and QPIP instead, with slightly different rates. All 2025 rates are embedded in these calculations.
