Global Take-Home Pay Report 2026: 14 Countries Compared

The same salary buys wildly different lives depending on where you earn it. This report measures exactly how much of a $50,000, $100,000 and $200,000 salary a single employee actually keeps in 14 major economies — after income tax and every mandatory social contribution.

Headline pay is easy to compare, but it hides the number that decides how you actually live: take-home pay. Income tax, social security, national insurance, pension levies and health contributions all take a slice before money reaches your account — and the size of that slice swings from zero to nearly half of gross depending on the country. This report converts a fixed dollar salary into each country's local currency, applies that country's real 2026 tax logic, and converts the net result back to US dollars so every figure is directly comparable.

All numbers are produced from Tabutility's live salary and income-tax calculators — the same code that powers each country's dedicated tool — and baked into this page at build time. Nothing here is a rounded average or a third-party estimate: open any calculator, enter the same salary, and you will get the same answer. We use a single-employee profile with no voluntary deductions throughout, so the comparison is apples to apples.

Executive summary

Five headline findings from the 2026 data. Each is computed directly from the figures in this report.

  • A $100,000 earner keeps about $30,839 more per year in Singapore than in Germany — 80% take-home versus just 49%.
  • The UAE keeps 100% of gross at every salary level, while Germany keeps the least everywhere — only 49% at $100k and 52% at $200k.
  • Ireland punishes progression hardest: its effective deduction climbs 23 percentage points between $50k and $200k, the steepest jump of any country compared.
  • Singapore is the great outlier — thanks to its capped CPF, its take-home actually rises from 76% at $50k to 81% at $200k, overtaking the US among taxed countries.
  • On a flat $100,000 salary, the gap between the highest and lowest take-home is roughly $50,518 a year — the same job, 5052 percentage points of pay apart depending on where you sign the contract.

The through-line: where you earn matters as much as how much you earn. A mid-career professional relocating from Germany to Singapore on the same $100,000 package would see take-home jump by roughly $30,839 a year — before accounting for any difference in cost of living. Tax-free jurisdictions like the UAE sit in a category of their own, while high-benefit European systems trade cash today for public services and social insurance.

The squeeze zone

Which systems bite hardest as you climb the salary ladder? We measured the change in effective rate — the total share lost to tax and social contributions — between $50,000 and $200,000. The bigger the jump, the more a raise gets "squeezed" by progressive taxation. The UAE is excluded (its rate is zero at every level).

Ireland, Netherlands and United Kingdom squeeze high earners hardest, with effective rates climbing 23, 23 and 21 percentage points respectively across the $50k–$200k range. These systems combine low higher-rate thresholds with steep marginal bands, so a promotion is taxed hard.

At the other extreme, 🇸🇬 Singapore is the flattest of the taxed countries, with its effective rate rising only -4.8 percentage points across the same range — the mark of a system whose contributions are heavily capped. Germany, despite finishing last overall, is not in the squeeze top three: its burden is already high at $50,000, so there is less room for it to climb.

Methodology

Every net-pay figure in this report is produced by porting the exact tax logic from Tabutility's individual country calculators. Each country uses the latest tax year implemented in its live tool as of August 2026 — the UK figures use 2025/26, Canada uses its 2026 federal brackets, Singapore uses YA 2025, and so on. These are not rounded averages or third-party estimates; they match what you get by opening each calculator and entering the same salary.

To keep the comparison fair we apply each tool's default single-employee assumptions: one person, employed (not self-employed), with no pension, retirement or voluntary contributions and no dependants. Where a country's default requires a specific choice, we state it. The US uses single filing status in Texas, a state with no income tax, so the US figure excludes state income tax — a high-tax state such as California would keep noticeably less. Germany uses tax class 1. Australia treats employer superannuation as a separate on-top cost, not a payslip deduction, so it is not subtracted from take-home. India uses the new tax regime and, exactly like the source tool, reflects income tax and 4% cess only — it does not model the Employees' Provident Fund. The UAE figure assumes an expat employee, for whom there is no personal income tax and no mandatory social contribution.

Salaries are entered in US dollars and converted to each local currency using a fixed reference exchange-rate table as of August 2026: United States — 1 USD = 1 US$; United Kingdom — 1 USD = 0.79 £; Australia — 1 USD = 1.55 A$; Canada — 1 USD = 1.38 C$; Ireland — 1 USD = 0.92 €; New Zealand — 1 USD = 1.68 NZ$; Germany — 1 USD = 0.92 €; Netherlands — 1 USD = 0.92 €; Singapore — 1 USD = 1.34 S$; South Africa — 1 USD = 18 R; Spain — 1 USD = 0.92 €; Japan — 1 USD = 150 ¥; India — 1 USD = 87 ₹; United Arab Emirates — 1 USD = 3.67 AED. Net pay is calculated in local currency and converted back to USD. Because the rates are fixed rather than live, the ranking stays stable over time; for an exact conversion on any given day, check a live currency service. All figures are estimates for comparison and general information — they are not personal tax advice.

Per-country tax years used: United States (2026 federal brackets); United Kingdom (2025/26); Australia (2025–26); Canada (2026); Ireland (2025); New Zealand (2025); Germany (2025); Netherlands (2025 (Box 1)); Singapore (YA 2025); South Africa (2025/26); Spain (2025); Japan (2025); India (FY 2024-25 (new regime)); United Arab Emirates (n/a (no income tax)).

Frequently asked questions

Which country has the highest take-home pay in 2026?

The UAE keeps 100% of gross pay for expat employees because there is no personal income tax. Among countries that do tax income, Singapore leads at higher salaries (80% kept on $100,000 thanks to its capped CPF), followed by the United States. Germany keeps the least at every level, retaining 49% on $100,000.

How much of a $100,000 salary do you keep after tax?

It ranges enormously. On $100,000 gross a single employee keeps $80,321 in Singapore, $79,180 in the United States and $49,482 in Germany — a spread of about $50,518 a year for the same salary.
