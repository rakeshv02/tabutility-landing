The Same Job. 50 Different Paychecks.

We ran a $75,000 gross salary through every state's 2024 tax system. The gap between the highest and lowest take-home is over $6,000 a year — from the same salary, doing the same work.

State income tax is the biggest variable in your paycheck that most people ignore when evaluating a job offer. On a $75,000 salary, federal taxes are identical everywhere — $8,990 in federal income tax plus $5,738 in FICA. But state income tax swings from $0 in nine states to over $4,000 in California and Oregon. That's a $4,000+ swing that compounds every single year you stay in your state.

Below is the complete breakdown: every state ranked by take-home pay, the methodology behind the numbers, and what it means for your career and financial planning.

    Written analysis

    The Hidden Variable in Every Job Offer

    When you evaluate a $75,000 job offer in Wyoming versus a $75,000 offer in California, you're not comparing equal paychecks. Wyoming residents take home approximately $60,272 annually. California residents take home approximately $54,041 — a difference of over $6,200 per year from the identical gross salary. Over 10 years, that gap exceeds $62,000.

    Most job seekers focus on gross salary. Financial advisors, tax professionals, and anyone who has moved between states for work know the reality: your effective compensation depends as much on where you work as what you earn.

    Why Nine States Give You a Free Pass

    Alaska, Florida, Nevada, South Dakota, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming impose no income tax at all — on any income. New Hampshire and Tennessee don't tax wages either (they historically taxed investment income, but both have eliminated even that). For a $75,000 earner, this is worth roughly $2,500–$4,500 per year compared to the average state.

    But "no income tax" doesn't always mean "low total taxes." These states often compensate through higher property taxes, sales taxes, or other fees. Texas, for example, has no income tax but relatively high property taxes. Still, for a renter earning $75,000, a no-income-tax state is an unambiguous financial win.

    The Middle Tier: Most States Cluster Around $56,000–$58,000

    The majority of states — roughly 30 of the 51 jurisdictions — deliver take-home pay between $56,000 and $59,000 on a $75,000 salary. States with flat income taxes (like Illinois at 4.95% or Michigan at 4.25%) are predictable and near the middle of the pack. States with progressive taxes tend to spread across a wider range depending on where $75,000 falls in their bracket structure.

    Utah's 4.65% flat rate keeps it competitive. Pennsylvania's 3.07% flat rate makes it one of the best-value states despite not being in the no-income-tax club. Indiana, at 3.05%, is similarly efficient.

    The High-Tax States: Where the Gap Hurts Most

    California, Hawaii, New Jersey, Oregon, and Minnesota round out the bottom of the rankings. At $75,000, California's progressive brackets push the effective state rate toward 5.9% — not the top 13.3% rate that gets headlines, but still substantial. California also has a lower standard deduction ($5,202) than the federal $14,600, meaning more of your income is taxable at the state level.

    Oregon's income tax structure is similarly aggressive for middle earners: the 8.75% bracket kicks in at $17,400 for single filers. At $75,000, after Oregon's standard deduction, a large portion of income falls in that bracket, creating an effective state rate around 7%.

    What the Numbers Mean for Career Decisions

    The $6,000+ annual gap between top and bottom states equals:

    • A $500/month difference in take-home pay — enough to cover most car payments
    • Over 8% of the gross salary itself — effectively a hidden tax on choosing to live in a high-tax state
    • $60,000+ over a decade, which at a 7% return in an index fund would grow to over $83,000

    Remote work has made state selection more flexible than ever. Many remote workers are legally permitted to establish residency in a no-income-tax state without changing their employer. But state tax residency rules are complex — spending more than 183 days in a state generally makes you a resident for tax purposes, and some states (notably New York and California) aggressively pursue tax claims on former residents.

    The Federal Floor Everyone Shares

    Regardless of state, every American earning $75,000 pays the same federal taxes in 2024. After the $14,600 standard deduction, taxable income is $60,400. Federal income tax at 2024 brackets is $8,990. FICA (Social Security 6.2% + Medicare 1.45%) adds $5,737.50. That's $14,727.50 in unavoidable federal deductions — 19.6% of gross — before a single state dollar is collected.

    This federal baseline means the maximum possible take-home on $75,000 — achievable only in a no-income-tax state — is $60,272. No state can help you do better than that without deductions. The question is simply how much your state adds on top of the federal obligation.

    Methodology

    All figures use the following assumptions and sources:

    • Filing status: Single filer
    • Gross salary: $75,000
    • Federal standard deduction (2024): $14,600 → taxable income $60,400
    • Federal income tax (2024 brackets): 10% on $0–$11,600; 12% on $11,601–$47,150; 22% on $47,151–$100,525. At $60,400 taxable: $8,990
    • Social Security: 6.2% on wages up to $168,600 wage base → $4,650 on $75,000
    • Medicare: 1.45% on all wages (no cap) → $1,087.50 on $75,000
    • State income tax: 2024 rates from each state's revenue department. State standard deductions applied where applicable. Progressive brackets computed accurately.
    • Not included: Local/city income taxes, 401(k) or other pre-tax deductions, state sales tax, property tax, health insurance premiums, or any tax credits beyond standard deduction.

    Frequently asked questions

    Which US states have no state income tax?

    Nine states collect no state income tax on wages: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire (taxes only investment income, not wages), South Dakota, Tennessee (taxes only investment income), Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. Residents of these states keep more of each paycheck compared to high-tax states like California and Hawaii, though they may face other taxes like higher sales or property taxes.

    How much federal income tax do you pay on $75,000?

    On a $75,000 gross salary in 2024, a single filer using the standard deduction ($14,600) has taxable income of $60,400. Federal income tax comes to $8,990: 10% on the first $11,600 ($1,160), 12% on the next $35,550 ($4,266), and 22% on the remaining $13,250 ($2,915). Add FICA (Social Security 6.2% = $4,650 + Medicare 1.45% = $1,088) and total federal obligations are $14,728 for every American regardless of state.

    What is the take-home pay difference between the best and worst state on $75,000?

    The gap between the state with the highest take-home pay (no-income-tax states like Wyoming, Texas, Florida etc.) and the state with the lowest (California, with its high progressive rates) is approximately $5,900 to $6,200 per year on a $75,000 salary. Over a 10-year career, that difference compounds to over $60,000 in additional take-home pay.

    Why does California have the lowest take-home pay?

    California has the highest marginal state income tax rate in the country (13.3% at the top, though $75,000 falls in the 8%–9.3% bracket range). At $75,000, after applying California's $5,202 standard deduction, the state income tax bill is approximately $4,457. Combined with federal taxes, California residents keep the smallest share of their $75,000 paycheck.

    Does this calculation include Social Security and Medicare taxes?

    Yes. FICA taxes are included for all 51 jurisdictions (50 states + DC). Social Security is 6.2% on wages up to $168,600 (2024 wage base), so on $75,000 it's $4,650. Medicare is 1.45% on all wages with no cap, so on $75,000 it's $1,087.50. These are the same for everyone regardless of state, totaling $5,737.50 in FICA taxes per year.
